**Short answer:** you can deduct **no more than $25 per recipient per year** for business gifts to clients, customers, and other business contacts. That is the rule in [IRS Publication 463](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p463), chapter 3. Send a client an $80 gift basket and only $25 of it is deductible. Wrapping, packaging, insurance, and shipping usually don't count toward the $25, so you can deduct those on top. Gifts to **employees** follow a different set of rules: a low-value holiday item such as a ham or a turkey can be tax-free to them, but **cash and gift cards are always taxable wages**, however small.

![What counts toward the IRS $25 business gift limit: the gift itself counts, per recipient per year; wrapping, packaging, insurance and mailing usually don't; imprinted items of $4 or less handed out widely, and signs or displays used at the recipient's business, are not treated as gifts](assets/images/business-gifts-25-limit-what-counts.webp "The $25 limit applies to the gift itself, per person, per tax year. Source: IRS Publication 463 (2025), chapter 3.")

This guide is for sole proprietors, single-member LLCs, and small partnerships planning year-end gifts. It covers federal income tax only, using the current IRS publications: Publication 463 (2025 edition, for 2025 returns) and Publication 15-B (2026 edition). Your state may have its own rules, which we did not check.

# How the $25 limit works

Publication 463 puts it in one sentence: you can deduct no more than **$25 for business gifts you give directly or indirectly to each person during your tax year**. A few consequences follow from that wording.

- **It's per person, not per gift.** Two $20 gifts to the same client in one year are $40 of gifts, and you can deduct $25.
- **It's per tax year.** For most sole proprietors that's the calendar year, so a gift in late December and another in January fall in different years.
- **Gifts to a company can count as gifts to people.** A gift to a company that's meant for the personal use of a particular person, or a limited group of people, is treated as an indirect gift to each of those people.
- **Gifts to a client's family count as gifts to the client**, unless you have a genuine, independent business connection with that family member and the gift isn't meant for the client.
- **You and your spouse count as one giver.** This applies even if you each run separate businesses. A partnership and its partners also count as one giver.

The IRS's own example: you and your spouse give a customer company three $80 gift baskets ($240 total), and three executives take them home for their families. You can deduct **$75**, which is $25 for each of the three people.

# What doesn't count toward the $25

**Incidental costs.** Engraving on jewelry, packaging, insuring, and mailing are generally left out when you figure the gift's cost. A cost is incidental only if it doesn't add substantial value. Gift wrap is incidental. A decorative basket that's worth a lot compared with the fruit inside it isn't, so its cost counts.

**Two kinds of items aren't treated as gifts at all**, so the limit doesn't apply to them:

1. An item that costs **$4 or less**, has your name clearly and permanently printed on it, and is one of many identical items you hand out widely. The IRS's examples are pens, desk sets, and plastic bags and cases.
2. **Signs, display racks, or other promotional material** for use on the recipient's business premises.

# Gift or entertainment? Tickets are the trap

Holiday tickets to a game or a show look like a gift, but Publication 463 says anything that could be considered either a gift or entertainment is **generally treated as entertainment**. Entertainment expenses are generally **not deductible at all** under the same publication. So event tickets are usually a worse choice for tax purposes than a $25 gift.

There's one carve-out the IRS spells out: **packaged food or beverages** you give a customer to use later are treated as a gift. A box of cookies or a bottle of wine shipped to a client falls under the $25 rule, not the entertainment rule.

# Gifts to employees follow different rules

The $25 rule in Publication 463 is about business contacts. For your own employees, the question is mostly whether the gift is **taxable wages**, and that's covered in [Publication 15-B](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p15b), the employer's guide to fringe benefits.

![Client gifts versus employee gifts: client gifts are deductible up to $25 per person per year; for employees, low-value non-cash holiday gifts can be excluded from wages as de minimis benefits, but cash, gift cards and gift certificates are always taxable wages; tangible length-of-service or safety awards have separate $400 and $1,600 limits](assets/images/business-gifts-client-vs-employee.webp "Client gifts are limited by the deduction rule; employee gifts are mainly a payroll question. Sources: IRS Publication 463 (2025); IRS Publication 15-B (2026).")

- **Low-value holiday gifts other than cash can be tax-free.** Publication 15-B lists "holiday or birthday gifts, other than cash, with a low fair market value" as an example of a de minimis benefit you can leave out of the employee's wages. It doesn't set a dollar figure.
- **Cash and gift cards never qualify.** Cash and cash equivalents, including gift certificates and gift cards, are never excludable as de minimis benefits, no matter how little they're worth. Treat a $50 holiday gift card like a $50 bonus: it goes through payroll and onto the employee's Form W-2.
- **Length-of-service and safety awards have their own limits.** Tangible personal property given as an achievement award for length of service or safety can be excluded up to **$400** for nonqualified awards, or **$1,600** in total including qualified plan awards. Cash, gift cards, vacations, meals, and event tickets don't qualify for this exclusion.

If you don't have employees yet and pay contractors instead, a gift to a contractor is generally a gift to a business contact under the $25 rule. Contractor payments themselves are a separate question, covered in our guide to [[how-to-e-file-1099-nec-iris|e-filing Form 1099-NEC with IRIS]].

# Quick comparison

| Gift | Tax treatment |
|---|---|
| $60 gift basket to one client | $25 deductible; shipping and gift wrap deductible on top |
| Branded pens costing $3 each, handed out widely | Not a gift for the $25 limit |
| Tickets to a game for a client | Generally entertainment, which is generally not deductible |
| Box of chocolates shipped to a client's office for one contact | Packaged food for later use, so a gift: $25 limit |
| Holiday ham for each employee | Can be a de minimis benefit, not wages |
| $50 gift card for each employee | Taxable wages, run through payroll |

# Keep simple records

Publication 463's recordkeeping table lists three things to keep for each gift: **its cost, the date, and a description**. Adding the recipient's name and why you gave it makes the per-person limit easy to show. You don't always need every recipient's name: a general description is enough when it's clear you aren't trying to get around the $25 limit, such as when you give one or two inexpensive tickets each to many customers. Sole proprietors report gift expenses on Schedule C.

# Year-end gift checklist

- List each client or contact you plan to send a gift to this year, including earlier gifts to the same person.
- Budget the deduction at $25 per person, plus shipping and wrapping.
- Skip event tickets if the deduction matters to you; choose packaged food or a physical item.
- For employees, give non-cash items of low value, or run cash and gift cards through payroll.
- Keep receipts showing the cost, date, and description of each gift, plus who received it.
