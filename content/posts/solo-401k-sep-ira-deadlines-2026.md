**Short answer:** if you're self-employed with no employees and want to put part of your 2026 profit into a retirement plan, a **SEP-IRA** can still be opened and funded for 2026 as late as your tax return due date, **including extensions** (October 15, 2027 if you extend). A **Solo 401(k)** lets you put away more at the same profit, because it adds a **$24,500 salary deferral** for 2026 on top of the employer contribution. If you want those 2026 deferrals, the safest move is to **adopt the plan by December 31, 2026**. A newer rule lets a sole proprietor adopt a brand-new Solo 401(k) as late as **April 15, 2027**, but the deferrals must be paid in by then too.

This guide is for sole proprietors and single-member LLC owners who file Schedule C, have **no employees**, and are deciding before year-end. It covers federal rules only. It doesn't cover partnerships, S corporations, or plans for businesses with staff, and it doesn't pick investments or providers.

# The two plans in one paragraph each

**SEP-IRA.** A Simplified Employee Pension is an employer plan funded only by **employer contributions**. As a self-employed person you are the employer. You set up a SEP (many people use the IRS model, **Form 5305-SEP**) and contribute to a SEP IRA at a bank, brokerage, or other financial institution. There is no salary deferral piece, and IRS Publication 560 says no new SARSEPs (the old salary reduction version) can be set up after 1996.

**Solo 401(k).** The IRS calls it a **one-participant 401(k) plan**. It's a regular 401(k) plan that covers a business owner with no employees, or the owner and their spouse. The IRS says the owner "wears two hats," so you can contribute both as the **employee** (elective deferrals) and as the **employer** (nonelective contributions). You have to adopt a **written plan**, usually an IRS pre-approved plan document from a provider.

# The 2026 limits

These are the IRS's published 2026 numbers. They come from Notice 2025-67 and the IRS cost-of-living table.

| 2026 limit | SEP-IRA | Solo 401(k) |
|---|---|---|
| Salary (elective) deferral | Not available | **$24,500** |
| Catch-up, age 50 or over | Not available | **$8,000** |
| Higher catch-up, ages 60 to 63 | Not available | **$11,250** (instead of $8,000) |
| Employer contribution for yourself | 20% of net earnings (explained below) | Same 20% calculation |
| Overall cap, not counting catch-ups | **$72,000** | **$72,000** |
| Compensation that can be counted | Up to **$360,000** | Up to **$360,000** |

The IRS's 401(k) limits page puts the Solo 401(k) total another way. Annual additions can't exceed the lesser of 100% of compensation or $72,000, which becomes $80,000 with the regular catch-up and up to $83,250 for ages 60 to 63.

The deferral limit is **per person, not per plan**. If you also have a 401(k) at a day job, your deferrals there and in your Solo 401(k) share the same $24,500.

# Why "20%" and not "25%"

Both plans describe the employer contribution as up to **25% of compensation**. For a self-employed person, compensation is your **net earnings from self-employment** after you subtract half of your self-employment tax and the contribution for yourself. Because the contribution is subtracted from the base it's figured on, a 25% plan rate works out to **20%** of net earnings before that contribution.

Publication 560's **Rate Table for Self-Employed** shows the conversion directly. A 25% plan rate becomes a 0.200000 self-employed rate. Its footnote says the deduction for employer contributions to a SEP or profit-sharing plan can't be more than **20% of your net earnings** (figured without deducting contributions for yourself) from the business that has the plan.

# A worked example

The numbers below are **our own arithmetic** for a hypothetical owner. They're rounded and assume nothing else (no other plans, no day-job 401(k)). Use the Deduction Worksheet for Self-Employed in Publication 560, chapter 5, for your real figures.

![Hypothetical sole proprietor under 50 with $60,000 profit in 2026: a SEP-IRA allows about $11,152, which is 20% of $55,761 net earnings; a Solo 401(k) allows about $35,652, made up of a $24,500 salary deferral plus the same $11,152 employer contribution](assets/images/solo-401k-sep-60k-example.webp "Illustration only, our arithmetic. Rate from the IRS Pub. 560 (2025) rate table; deferral limit from the IRS 2026 COLA table.")

- Schedule C net profit: **$60,000**. The owner is under 50.
- Self-employment tax: $60,000 × 92.35% × 15.3% ≈ **$8,478**. Half of it, about **$4,239**, is deductible.
- Net earnings for the plan calculation: $60,000 − $4,239 ≈ **$55,761**.
- **SEP-IRA:** 20% × $55,761 ≈ **$11,152**.
- **Solo 401(k):** the same $11,152 employer contribution, plus up to **$24,500** in elective deferrals, for about **$35,652**. That's under the $72,000 cap and under 100% of compensation.

At this profit level the Solo 401(k) ceiling is roughly three times the SEP's. At much higher profits the gap narrows, because both plans hit the same $72,000 overall cap.

# The deadlines, plan by plan

## SEP-IRA: the most forgiving

Publication 560 says you can **set up a SEP for any year as late as the due date (including extensions)** of your income tax return for that year. To deduct contributions for a year, you must **make them by the due date (including extensions)** of that return.

For a calendar-year Form 1040 filer, that means:

- **April 15, 2027** if you don't extend.
- **October 15, 2027** if you file an extension.

This is why a SEP is the usual fallback when someone realizes in March that they had a good year and never opened a plan.

## Solo 401(k): two separate questions

For a Solo 401(k), split the question in two: when the **plan** has to exist, and when the **money** has to go in.

**When the money goes in.** Publication 560's Table 1 lists the last date for both elective deferrals and profit-sharing employer contributions as the **due date of the employer's return, including extensions**. Its footnote says some plans covered by Department of Labor rules have earlier deposit deadlines for deferrals, but that solo/self-employed 401(k) plans are **non-ERISA plans** and don't fall under those rules.

**When the plan has to exist.** Here Publication 560 (2025) gives two statements that don't fully line up:

- **Table 1** says a defined contribution plan can be set up **by the employer's tax-filing due date, including extensions**, for the tax year.
- The **Set-up deadline** paragraph in chapter 4 says that to deduct contributions for a tax year, the plan must be adopted **by the last day of that year**. It then adds the exception below.

**The late-adoption exception for sole proprietors.** Under the SECURE 2.0 Act, Publication 560 says that for 2023 and later years, **a sole proprietor with no employees can adopt a 401(k) plan after the end of the tax year**, as long as it's adopted **by the tax filing deadline without extensions**. Elective deferrals for that first plan year must be **paid to the plan before the return's due date, again without extensions**, to be treated as made by the end of the first plan year.

For a 2026 calendar-year return, that filing deadline is **April 15, 2027**. An extension doesn't stretch it.

**What we'd take from this.** We couldn't reconcile the two statements from the IRS text alone, so here's the plan that satisfies both:

1. If you want to make **2026 salary deferrals**, adopt the Solo 401(k) **by December 31, 2026**. That date works under either reading.
2. If you miss December 31 and you're a **sole proprietor** with no employees, the late-adoption rule still gives you until **April 15, 2027**. You have to adopt the plan *and* get the deferral money into it by that date.
3. If you're a partnership or corporation, don't rely on the sole-proprietor exception. Publication 560 describes it only for sole proprietors.
4. If all you want for 2026 is an employer-style contribution and the deadline has passed for a Solo 401(k), a **SEP** set up by your extended due date is the clearly documented option.

Plan providers also set their own paperwork cutoffs, often well before December 31. Check yours early.

# Which one fits you

| Your situation | Usually simpler choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Profit is modest and you want to save as much as possible | Solo 401(k) | The $24,500 deferral isn't tied to the 20% calculation |
| You're deciding after December 31 and missed the Solo 401(k) window | SEP-IRA | Can be set up through the extended due date |
| You want minimal paperwork | SEP-IRA | Model Form 5305-SEP usually means no annual plan return |
| You expect to hire employees soon | Look at both carefully | Employees who become eligible must be covered under either plan |
| You already max out a 401(k) at a day job | SEP-IRA or the employer piece of a Solo 401(k) | Deferral limit is per person |

On hiring: the IRS's one-participant 401(k) page says the no-testing advantage disappears once you hire employees who meet the plan's eligibility rules, because they must be included. Publication 560 says a SEP IRA must also be set up for each eligible employee.

# Paperwork after the plan exists

- **Deduction.** Sole proprietors deduct contributions for themselves on **Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 16**, according to Publication 560.
- **Form 5500-EZ (Solo 401(k) only).** The instructions say a one-participant plan doesn't have to file if total assets of all your one-participant plans are **$250,000 or less** at the end of the plan year, unless it's the plan's final year. Above that, file by the **last day of the 7th month** after the plan year ends. For a 2026 calendar-year plan, that's July 31, 2027, a Saturday, so it moves to the next business day.
- **SEP.** Publication 560 says to keep the original Form 5305-SEP and not file it with the IRS, and that using the model form usually relieves you from filing annual retirement plan information returns with the IRS and the Department of Labor.

# Year-end checklist

- Estimate your 2026 Schedule C profit and half of your self-employment tax.
- Decide whether you want salary deferrals. If yes, **adopt a Solo 401(k) by December 31, 2026**, and ask the provider for its own cutoff.
- If you won't need deferrals, a SEP can wait until you know your final numbers. Note April 15 and October 15, 2027.
- Check whether you contributed to a 401(k) at another job this year, so your deferrals stay under $24,500 in total.
- Keep the signed plan document (Solo 401(k)) or Form 5305-SEP (SEP).
- Calendar Form 5500-EZ only if Solo 401(k) assets may exceed $250,000.
