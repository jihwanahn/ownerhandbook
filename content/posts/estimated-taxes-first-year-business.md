**Short answer:** maybe not. If you were a U.S. citizen or resident for all of 2025 and your 2025 total tax was zero, you don't have to pay any estimated tax for 2026, even if your new business made money. Otherwise you generally must pay if you expect to owe **$1,000 or more** for 2026 after withholding and refundable credits, unless your withholding and on-time payments cover the smaller of **90% of your 2026 tax** or **100% of your 2025 tax**. If you still owe, the last 2026 payment is due **January 15, 2027**.

This guide covers federal estimated tax for sole proprietors, single-member LLC owners, partners, and S corporation shareholders, the people who use Form 1040-ES. States run their own estimated tax rules, so check your state revenue department separately.

# Why a new business owner suddenly owes estimated tax

When you work for an employer, tax is withheld from every paycheck. Business profit has no withholding. The IRS says you generally have to make estimated payments if you expect to owe at least $1,000 when you file, and Form 1040-ES lists income from self-employment among the income it is meant for.

Business profit also brings **self-employment tax**, the Social Security and Medicare tax your employer used to split with you. According to the 2026 Form 1040-ES worksheet:

- SE tax is figured on **92.35%** of your net profit.
- The rate is **12.4%** for Social Security, on earnings up to **$184,500** for 2026, plus **2.9%** for Medicare with no cap.
- You can deduct **half** of your SE tax when you figure income tax.

That's why a first profitable year can produce a surprisingly large bill, even when the profit is small.

# The three tests, one at a time

**1. Did you owe nothing for 2025?** You don't have to pay estimated tax for 2026 if all three of these are true:

- you had no tax liability for 2025;
- you were a U.S. citizen or resident alien for all of 2025;
- your 2025 tax year covered 12 months.

"No tax liability" means your 2025 total tax was zero, or you didn't have to file a return. This is the test that most often helps people who quit school, a job, or caregiving to start a business in 2026.

**2. Will you owe less than $1,000?** If you expect your 2026 balance due, after withholding and refundable credits, to be under $1,000, you don't need to make estimated payments.

**3. Do the safe harbors cover you?** You're covered if your withholding plus on-time payments reach the smaller of these:

- **90%** of the tax on your 2026 return;
- **100%** of the tax on your 2025 return (that return must cover all 12 months).

If your 2025 AGI was more than $150,000 ($75,000 if married filing separately), use **110%** of your 2025 tax in place of 100%. For many first-year owners, matching last year's tax is easier than predicting this year's profit.

**Still have a W-2 job?** The IRS lets you raise the withholding on that job instead. You do that by giving your employer a new Form W-4.

# 2026 due dates

![Timeline of 2026 estimated tax due dates: April 15, June 15, September 15, 2026 and January 15, 2027](assets/images/estimated-tax-2026-dates.webp "The four 2026 due dates. The January payment can be skipped only if you file and pay in full by February 1, 2027.")

| Payment | Due date |
|---|---|
| 1st | April 15, 2026 |
| 2nd | June 15, 2026 |
| 3rd | September 15, 2026 |
| 4th | January 15, 2027 |

- You can pay all of your 2026 estimated tax by April 15, 2026, or in four installments by the dates above.
- You can skip the January 15, 2027 payment if you file your 2026 return by **February 1, 2027** and pay the entire balance due with it.
- If a due date falls on a weekend or legal holiday, it moves to the next business day.
- A mailed payment counts as on time if it's postmarked by the due date.

# Started mid-year? What the penalty really looks like

The underpayment penalty is figured **separately for each due date**, for the number of days a payment stays short. It can apply even if you end up getting a refund. So a payment on January 15 doesn't erase a shortfall from June or September, but it stops the clock on any further days late.

There are two things that can help:

- **The annualized income installment method.** If your income arrived unevenly, for example because you opened in the fall, this method can lower or eliminate the penalty for the early periods. You figure it on Form 2210 and its Schedule AI.
- **Waivers.** The IRS can waive the penalty if the underpayment came from a casualty, disaster, or other unusual circumstance. It can also waive it if you retired after reaching age 62, or became disabled, in 2026 or 2025, and the underpayment was due to reasonable cause.

# How to figure the amount

1. Estimate your 2026 net business profit (income minus deductible expenses). Our [[llc-cost-breakdown|LLC cost breakdown]] lists the formation and annual fees most new owners pay.
2. Use the **2026 Estimated Tax Worksheet** in Form 1040-ES. It includes the SE tax and the 2026 standard deduction: $16,100 single, $32,200 married filing jointly, $24,150 head of household.
3. Compare the result with the safe-harbor amounts from the three tests above. Pay enough to meet the smaller one.
4. Subtract any withholding and payments you've already made, and spread the rest over the remaining due dates.

If you pay contractors, you'll also have year-end paperwork due soon. See [[do-i-need-to-send-1099-to-llc|whether you need to send a 1099 to an LLC]].

# How to pay

The IRS accepts estimated payments by any of these methods:

- **IRS Online Account** (IRS.gov/Account), where you can also see the payments you've already made.
- **IRS Direct Pay**, free, straight from a checking or savings account.
- **Debit card, credit card, or digital wallet** through an approved processor, which charges a fee.
- **EFTPS** (the Electronic Federal Tax Payment System). You have to enroll first.
- **Check or money order**, mailed with a Form 1040-ES payment voucher.

You don't have to stick to four payments. The IRS says you can pay weekly, biweekly, or monthly, as long as enough is paid by the end of each quarter. If you're also setting up business banking and IRS accounts, see [[ein-number-free|our EIN guide]].

**Corporations are different.** A C corporation generally makes its own estimated payments if it expects to owe $500 or more, using different rules and forms.
