Starting an LLC looks like one fee. In practice it is four, and two of them arrive
again every year whether or not the business earned anything.

That is the whole story of LLC pricing: a one-off filing charge that every comparison
article leads with, and a set of recurring charges that decide what the thing actually
costs you by year three.

This guide adds up all four lines with 2026 numbers, taken from the agencies that
charge them, and shows where the gap between a $50 LLC and a $1,600 one comes from.

If you remember one thing: budget the annual lines first. The filing fee is the
smallest decision you make.

# The four costs, in order

| Cost | Typical range | Recurring? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| State filing fee | $35 – $500 | No (one-off) |
| Registered agent | $0 – $250 / year | Yes |
| Annual report or state renewal | $0 – $800 / year | Yes |
| Business bank account | $0 – $360 / year | Yes |

![LLC state filing fees, 2026, six examples](assets/images/llc-filing-fee-breakdown.webp "Most states charge between $50 and $200; Massachusetts is the outlier at $500.")

The filing fee is the number every comparison article leads with, and it is the least
interesting one — it is paid once. The other three repeat, which is why two LLCs with
the same filing fee can cost wildly different amounts by year three.

# What year one actually adds up to

Four lines, one sum:

- **Filing fee** — what the secretary of state charges to accept the paperwork. Paid once.
- **Registered agent** — $0 when a member of the LLC does it, up to about $250 a year
  when you hire a service.
- **Annual report or state renewal** — $0 in some states, $500 in Massachusetts, and
  $800 in California as a minimum franchise tax.
- **Business bank account** — $0 at an online-only bank, up to about $360 a year at a
  traditional one.

The cheapest honest total is a filing fee and almost nothing else: $35 in Montana or $40
in Kentucky, no annual report in states such as Arizona or New Mexico, and an
online-only bank account.

The most expensive version of the same four lines is roughly $1,500 a year — California's
$70 filing plus its $800 minimum, a $250 agent and a traditional bank account at $30 a
month. Same paperwork, same one-person business.

That spread is not a pricing trick. It is four independent choices, and three of them
are yours to make.

# The filing fee is the least interesting number

States set the fee themselves, for revenue as much as for administration, which is why
neighbouring states differ by hundreds of dollars. Montana charges $35 and Kentucky $40
at the low end; Massachusetts charges $500 at the top. In between sit California at $70,
Wyoming at $100, Florida at $125, New York at $200 and Texas at $300.

Choosing a state by filing fee alone is the most expensive common mistake in the whole
process. California takes $70 to file and then $800 a year; a state with a $300 filing
fee and a $0 annual report is the cheaper home by year two.

The states that attract out-of-state formations are usually sold on privacy or tax
arguments that do not apply to a one-person service business operating somewhere else.
If you live and work in one state, forming there is normally the cheapest and simplest
option — one filing, one annual report, one address.

# Registered agent: $0 or $125–$250 a year

A registered agent is the person the state contacts when it needs to serve the LLC with
legal papers. The job is receiving mail and being findable. It is not a lawyer, and it
is not something you are required to buy.

Most states let a member of the LLC act as its own agent, which is the $0 line. The
conditions are the ones people miss: a physical street address in the state (not a PO
box), and availability during normal business hours. If you work from home and do not
mind your address being on the public record, you can do this yourself.

Commercial services run from about $125 (Northwest) through $199 (ZenBusiness) to $249
(LegalZoom) a year. They earn their fee in three situations: you form in a state you do
not live in, you move often, or you do not want your home address in a public database.
Otherwise it is a subscription for a mailbox.

# The annual report: the bill that arrives whether or not you earned anything

This is the line that turns a $150 LLC into a $400 LLC, and the one almost no pricing
page mentions.

Every state calls it something slightly different — annual report, annual list,
statement of information, or a franchise tax — and every state sets its own fee.
Arizona and New Mexico do not require an LLC annual report at all. Massachusetts
charges $500. California
charges $800 as a minimum franchise tax, which applies to every LLC organised there or
doing business there, profit or loss.

The deadline is usually the anniversary of your formation date, or a fixed date in your
formation month. Miss it and the penalties start small and grow; miss it long enough and
the state dissolves the LLC administratively, which costs far more to undo than the
report itself.

Put the date in your calendar the week you file. It is a two-minute task once a year,
and a several-hundred-dollar one if you forget.

# The business bank account: $0 or $180–$360 a year

A separate account is not just bookkeeping hygiene. Paying personal bills out of a
business account is the quickest way to give a court a reason to ignore the liability
shield you paid to set up.

Online-only banks charge nothing for a basic business checking account. Traditional
banks charge roughly $15 to $30 a month — Wells Fargo's entry business account is $15,
Bank of America's Business Advantage is $29.95 — unless you keep a minimum balance, and
that balance is money you cannot spend elsewhere, so the waiver is not free either.

For a business with a handful of transactions a month, the $0 end of the range is
usually the right answer.

# If you are comparing DIY against a formation service

DIY costs the state fee plus about an hour of your time. The form is short, it is online
in most states, and the instructions sit on the secretary of state's own site.

A formation service adds its own fee on top of the state's — from nothing on the
bare-bones tier (Bizee, Inc Authority, LegalZoom Basic, ZenBusiness) to around $300 for
a full package, with Northwest at $39 and LegalZoom Pro at $249 in between.

The free tiers are real, but read what they include: usually the filing itself, with the
registered agent, the operating agreement and the "expedited" EIN as paid add-ons. The
EIN is the one to know about — the IRS issues it free and directly, in minutes, so
paying a service to obtain one is paying for a form you can fill in yourself.

What you actually need on day one: the filed formation documents, an EIN, and — if there
is more than one owner — an operating agreement saying who owns what and what happens
when someone leaves. For a single-member LLC the agreement is still worth writing,
because banks, landlords and contracts occasionally ask to see it.

# What happens after you file

The filing is the receipt, not the finish line. Four things follow it, and the order
matters more than the paperwork.

1. Save the stamped formation documents. Banks, landlords and licensing bodies ask for
   them, and replacements cost money and time.
2. Get the EIN before you open an account — the bank will ask for it.
3. Then apply for the business bank account, with the formation documents and the EIN in
   hand.
4. Calendar the annual report for the month it is due next year, not the month you filed.

![The first 90 days after forming an LLC](assets/images/first-90-days-timeline.webp "The deadlines that matter are 30, 60 and 90 days after your formation date, not the day you file.")

Most of what goes wrong in year one is not a fee. It is a deadline nobody wrote down.

# What is not in this number

Four costs sit outside the table, and they are the ones that surprise people.

- **State income or franchise tax.** California's $800 is in the table because it is
  unavoidable; other states tax LLC income through the owners' returns, at rates that
  depend on where you live and how the LLC is taxed.
- **Sales tax registration.** If you sell taxable goods or services, registering and
  filing is a separate obligation with its own deadlines and penalties.
- **Newspaper publication.** New York and a small number of other states require new
  LLCs to publish a notice in an approved newspaper. The bill varies by county, it is
  not optional, and it is not part of the filing fee — check it before you file there.
- **Foreign qualification.** If you operate in a state other than the one you formed in
  — a warehouse, an office, sometimes a single employee — that state can require you to
  register there too, which means a second filing fee and a second annual report.

Add bookkeeping, tax preparation and any licence your industry requires, and the LLC's
paperwork cost is a floor rather than a total.

# How to check your own state in ten minutes

1. Search for "<state> secretary of state LLC filing fee" and open the `.gov` page, not
   the first sponsored result.
2. Find the fee schedule itself — usually a PDF or a table headed "filing fees" — and
   take the LLC formation line, not the general business registration line.
3. On the same site, look for "annual report", "annual list" or "franchise tax". If you
   cannot find it, call the number on the filing page; the answer takes two minutes and
   it is the number that matters most.
4. Write the four lines down, add them, and set a calendar reminder a month before the
   annual deadline.

# Questions owners ask before filing

**Is a free formation service actually free?**
The service line is free or near-free; the state fee never is. The free tiers make their
money on the registered agent subscription, the operating agreement template and the
EIN, all of which you can arrange yourself. Read the checkout total, not the headline.

**Do I need a registered agent if I am the only owner?**
Not if you have a physical address in the state and are willing to have it listed
publicly — you are your own agent. If you form in a state you do not live in, you need
one, because the state has to be able to reach a real person there.

**What happens if the LLC earns nothing?**
The annual report is still due, and in California the $800 minimum is still due.
Arizona and New Mexico require no annual report, which is part of why they are popular
with businesses that are not trading yet. A dormant LLC in Massachusetts or California
is not free to keep.

**Can I move an LLC to another state?**
Usually through a conversion or domestication, which means filings in both states and,
in practice, professional help. For a small LLC the cheaper route is often a new entity
in the new state and a clean close of the old one, especially if it has no contracts,
assets or history yet.

**Is the cheapest state the right state?**
Rarely. For most owners the formation state, the operating state and the tax state are
the same thing, and splitting them adds a second filing, a second annual report and a
registered agent you would not otherwise need.

# Bottom line

The LLC's real cost is not the filing fee. It is the filing fee plus three recurring
lines, and the recurring lines are where the money goes.

In a low-fee state with no annual report, doing everything yourself, the total can stay
under $100 a year. In Massachusetts or California, with a paid agent and a traditional
bank, the same business passes $1,500. Most owners sit somewhere between the two, and
the single number that decides which end they land on is the annual report.

Starting the entity is the cheap part. Keeping it clean — separate account, filed
report, an address that can receive mail — is what you are actually paying for.
