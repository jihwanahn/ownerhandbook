An EIN is the cheapest thing you will ever buy for your business, because you will
never pay for one. It is a nine-digit federal tax number the IRS issues, the number is
the same no matter how you ask for it, and the government does not charge a cent for
any of the four ways to get it.

That last part is worth stating plainly, because a lot of the internet is built on the
opposite impression. If you have been quoted a fee for "EIN filing", you have been
quoted a fee for typing your answers into a free government form.

This guide covers what the number actually is, who needs one, the four routes and how
long each really takes, the six ways applications go wrong, and what to do with the
number the day it arrives.

# What an EIN actually is

An Employer Identification Number is a nine-digit number the IRS assigns in the format
XX-XXXXXXX, and it identifies an entity's tax accounts — employers, corporations,
partnerships, trusts and estates all have one. That is the whole job description. It is
not a licence, it is not a registration, and it is not a credit score.

Three facts about it that decide most practical questions:

- **It comes from the IRS, not from your state.** Forming an LLC in Delaware and
  getting an EIN are two different filings with two different governments (see
  [Publication 1635](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1635.pdf), the IRS's own guide to
  the number).
- **It is permanent.** Once assigned, it is "that entity's permanent federal taxpayer
  ID number". The IRS cannot cancel it — the most it can do is deactivate it.
- **It is not a personal number.** The IRS is explicit: an EIN is for use in connection
  with your business activities only, and is not a replacement for your Social
  Security number.

An entity should have exactly one. If you have picked up more than one — usually from
applying twice, or from inheriting a number when you took over a business — the
Business and Specialty Tax Line at 800-829-4933 will tell you which one to use.

# Who needs a number — and who can skip it

The IRS lists the straightforward cases first. You need an EIN if you:

- have employees;
- will pay employment, excise or alcohol, tobacco and firearms taxes;
- withhold tax on income paid to a non-resident alien.

You also need one to operate a partnership, an LLC, a corporation, a tax-exempt
organisation, an estate, a trust, a retirement plan or a farmers' cooperative.

The interesting case is the single-member LLC, which is where most of the confusion
lives. A one-owner LLC is disregarded for federal income tax purposes by default: the
IRS says a sole proprietor who operates as an LLC does not need a separate EIN for the
LLC **unless** they have employees, owe excise tax, or elect to be taxed as a
corporation or S corp.

That is a tax rule, not a practical answer, and the same page gives the practical
answer:

> If you don't need an EIN for federal tax purposes, you can still request one for
> banking or state tax purposes.

You should request one. A business bank account application asks for it, state licence
and permit offices ask for it, and any client who has to issue you a 1099 will ask for
it on a W-9. It costs nothing, it is issued in the same session you apply, and having it
removes a whole class of arguments with institutions later.

# There are four routes, and three of them are slow

Every route below issues the same number, and none of them charges anything.

| Route | Who it is for | How long |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Online at irs.gov | U.S. or U.S. territory principal place of business | Issued immediately |
| Fax [Form SS-4](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-ss-4) to 855-641-6935 | Anyone | About 4 business days |
| Mail Form SS-4 to Cincinnati, OH 45999 | Anyone | About 4 weeks |
| Phone 267-941-1099 (not toll-free) | Principal place of business outside the U.S., Mon–Fri 6 a.m.–11 p.m. ET | Same call |

![How long each EIN application route takes](assets/images/ein-wait-times.webp "The online tool issues the number in the session you apply. Fax and mail are the fallbacks, and the gap between them is measured in weeks.")

Two details from the online tool's own page are worth knowing before you sit down:

- **It keeps office hours.** The tool runs Monday to Friday 6 a.m. to 1 a.m. the next
  day, Saturday 6 a.m. to 9 p.m., and Sunday 6 p.m. to midnight, Eastern time. A
  Sunday-morning attempt simply does not connect.
- **You cannot save and come back.** The session expires after 15 minutes of
  inactivity and you start over. Have the responsible party's Social Security or
  individual taxpayer ID number in front of you before you begin.

If your principal place of business is outside the United States, the online tool is
closed to you by design — those applicants call, fax or mail instead, and the phone
line is the fast option.

# The fee you may have been quoted

Here is the IRS, in the first line of its own instructions:

> Applying for an Employer Identification Number (EIN) is a free service offered by the
> Internal Revenue Service. Beware of websites on the Internet that charge for this free
> service.

And on the application tool itself: "Beware of websites that charge for an EIN. You
never have to pay a fee for an EIN."

A paid EIN service is not doing anything you cannot do. There is one issuer and one EIN
system, so what a reseller sends you is the identical nine digits the IRS would have
given you directly — no faster, no better, no different in any way that a bank or a
state agency can see. What you are buying is somebody else typing Form SS-4.

The charge usually hides inside a formation package, where "EIN" appears next to the
filing as an add-on or as an "expedited" upgrade. The same pattern shows up across the
[[llc-cost-breakdown|LLC cost lines]] — the state fee is unavoidable, the add-ons
generally are not.

# What the application actually checks

Four things decide whether the session ends with a number or an error message.

**1. That the entity already exists at state level.** The IRS asks you to form an LLC,
corporation or partnership with your state *before* applying, and says a missing state
registration can delay the EIN. Apply in the wrong order and you get a number that
describes an entity the state does not recognise yet.

**2. Who the responsible party is.** This is the person who owns or controls the entity
and manages its funds — usually the principal officer of a corporation, the general
partner of a partnership, or the owner of an LLC. The IRS requires a natural person:
an entity cannot be the responsible party (government entities excepted), and a nominee
cannot apply at all. Putting a formation service or a nominee on the application risks
your entity's information being disclosed to somebody unauthorised.

**3. The entity type**, because it determines which later filings you owe.

**4. Name and address formatting.** The IRS system accepts only letters A–Z, numbers
0–9, hyphens and ampersands in a business name. Symbols like `+`, `@` or `.` have to be
spelled out or replaced with a space; slashes become hyphens; apostrophes are removed.
Street addresses are capped at 35 characters, so a long suite number needs the USPS
standard abbreviation.

# Where applications actually stall

The failure modes are boring and almost entirely self-inflicted.

- **Applying before the state filing clears.** The most common one, and the IRS warns
  about it directly.
- **The wrong responsible party.** If a nominee was used to process your state filing,
  the responsible party still has to be identified before you apply.
- **A name the system will not take.** You will be stopped inside the session, not
  after it.
- **Timing out.** Fifteen minutes of inactivity, no save, start again.
- **A second application the same day.** The IRS limits issuance to one EIN per
  responsible party per day, a rule in force since 21 May 2012 that applies to online,
  fax and mail requests equally. Two businesses in one day means one of them waits.
- **Applying again for an entity that already has a number.** One entity, one EIN. If
  you are applying because you cannot find the first one, you need the lost-EIN route
  below, not a new application.

None of these is a paperwork problem you can pay to solve. They are all fixed by
answering the form correctly the first time.

# The day the number arrives

Print the confirmation letter and keep a PDF. It is the document banks, landlords and
licensing offices ask to see, and it is much easier to produce in week one than in month
nine.

Then split your follow-up work by what actually clears when:

**Immediately usable** — opening a bank account, applying for business licences, filing
a tax return by mail.

**Up to two weeks** — enrolment in the Taxpayer ID Number Matching Program, e-filing
tax returns, and making tax deposits or electronic payments through EFTPS. If you have
hired staff and set a payday inside that window, that is the deadline you should be
counting backwards from.

Two standing habits that need the number and pay for themselves:

- **Ask every contractor for a W-9 up front**, before the first payment, and keep it.
  Form 1099-NEC is due to the IRS and to the recipient by 31 January of the following
  year, and chasing addresses in January is the expensive version of that task.
- **Keep payroll filings on the calendar** the moment the first employee starts.

If the number goes missing later: the confirmation notice and the entity transcript are
both available, and 800-829-4933 answers Monday to Friday, 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. your local
time.

# Changing it, closing it, and the reporting you can ignore

**A new EIN is for ownership and structure changes.** Incorporate, form a partnership,
be acquired, become a subsidiary, terminate an LLC and start a corporation — new
number. Change your business name, move, add a location, or change the responsible
party — same number, and for the last three the IRS wants
[Form 8822-B](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f8822b.pdf) filed within 60 days.

**Closing down does not cancel the number.** The IRS cannot cancel an EIN; it can only
deactivate it, by letter, with your entity's legal name and address and a reason. File
outstanding returns first.

**FinCEN's beneficial ownership reporting no longer applies to U.S. companies.** If you
find older advice telling you to file a BOI report within 30 days of forming an LLC,
it is out of date: FinCEN's final rule, published in August 2026, exempts U.S. companies
and removes the requirement to report U.S. person beneficial owners or company
applicants. There is nothing to file.

# Questions owners ask

**Is an EIN the same as a tax ID number?**
For a business, yes — "federal tax ID", "EIN" and "FEIN" all refer to the same
nine-digit IRS number. It is not the same as a state tax registration number, which
comes from your state's revenue department and is a separate filing.

**Can I get an EIN without a Social Security number?**
You need the responsible party's Social Security number or individual taxpayer ID
number (ITIN) for the online application, so an ITIN holder can apply. If nobody
involved has either, the application has to go through the phone, fax or mail routes,
which allow additional documentation.

**Do I need the EIN before I can open a bank account?**
In practice, yes. The EIN appears on the SBA's own list of documents banks ask for when
opening a business account, alongside the formation documents, ownership agreements and
licences. Sole proprietors are usually the exception — banks can accept a Social
Security number — which is one more reason to get the EIN and stop having the
conversation.

**I have a single-member LLC and no employees. Is an EIN pointless?**
Not for federal income tax, where you can report on Schedule C under your own number.
But you can request one for banking and state tax purposes, and you will be asked for it
the first time you open an account, register for sales tax or sign a contract that
requires a W-9.

**How long does it really take?**
Online, minutes — the IRS issues the number during the session once the application is
validated. Fax, about four business days. Mail, about four weeks. The number itself is
free on every route.

# Bottom line

An EIN costs nothing, takes a few minutes to apply for, and exists so that every
business taxpayer can be identified. The only two ways to get it wrong are applying in
the wrong order — before your state filing clears — and paying somebody for it.

Do it yourself, print the confirmation, and file it with the formation documents. The
add-on fees in a business formation checkout are worth a separate look; the
[[llc-cost-breakdown|LLC cost breakdown]] covers which of those lines are real costs and
which are a charge for a free form.
