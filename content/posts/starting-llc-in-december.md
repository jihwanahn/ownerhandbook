**Short answer:** if you won't do any business until January, you usually don't have to choose. You can do the paperwork now and have the LLC start on January 1. **Florida** and **Texas** let you list a later **effective date** on your formation filing, so the company is on file in December but legally begins on January 1, 2027. In Florida, that pushes your first **$138.75 annual report** back a full year. **California** works differently. It has a **15-day rule**: an LLC whose first taxable year is 15 days or less, and that does no business in California during it, owes no **$800 annual tax** for that stub year. If you need the LLC working in December, file now and accept the extra year of state fees.

![Starting an LLC in December: Florida January 1 effective date moves the first annual report from 2027 to 2028; California filing on or after December 17 with no business avoids the 2026 $800 tax; Texas allows an effective date up to 90 days after signing](assets/images/llc-december-or-january.webp "Three state rules that make the December-or-January choice matter. Sources: Florida Division of Corporations, California FTB Pub. 3556, Texas SOS Form 205.")

This guide is for someone planning a new LLC late in the year, usually a single owner, sometimes two or three partners. Rules differ by state. We cover the three states whose official forms and pages we checked, plus the federal side that applies everywhere.

# Why the date matters at all

A state counts your LLC's years from the day it legally comes into existence. If that day is December 20, 2026, then 2026 is year one, even though it lasts 12 days. Annual reports, franchise taxes, and minimum taxes are often tied to that first year. So a December start can mean paying for a year in which you did almost nothing.

The federal side is calmer. For income tax, the IRS treats a **single-member LLC** as a "disregarded entity" unless it elects to be taxed as a corporation. Its activity goes on your own Form 1040, usually on **Schedule C**, so a December start doesn't create a separate federal return. A **multi-member LLC** is a partnership by default, which does change things (see below).

# Florida: list January 1 as the effective date

The Florida Division of Corporations spells this out in its instructions for Articles of Organization:

- An LLC's existence begins when the Division files your Articles, **unless** you list an effective date.
- The effective date can be up to **five business days before**, or **90 days after**, the date the Division receives your filing.
- If you form between **October 1 and December 31** and don't expect to do business until next year, the Division suggests listing **January 1** as the effective date. Your company is in its records right away, but its existence starts January 1.
- Annual reports are filed **January 1 to May 1** of the calendar year **after** the filing date, or after the effective date if you listed one.

![Florida LLC filed in December 2026: with a December effective date the first annual report is due January 1 to May 1, 2027; with a January 1, 2027 effective date it moves to January 1 to May 1, 2028](assets/images/llc-florida-effective-date.webp "Same filing day, different first report. The annual report is $138.75; after May 1 a $400 late fee is added. Source: Florida Division of Corporations, Form CR2E047 and Articles of Organization instructions.")

In practice, a Florida LLC filed in December 2026 without an effective date owes its first annual report between January 1 and May 1, **2027**. With a January 1, 2027 effective date, the first report isn't due until 2028. The report costs **$138.75**, and Florida adds a **$400 late fee** after May 1, with no waiver. The formation filing itself is $125 ($100 for the Articles plus $25 for the registered agent designation), whichever date you pick.

# California: the 15-day rule

Every California LLC that isn't taxed as a corporation owes an **$800 annual tax** for each taxable year, or part of one, once the Secretary of State accepts its Articles of Organization. That's true even if it does no business. The first-year exemption only covered LLCs formed in 2021 through 2023, so it doesn't help a 2026 or 2027 filing.

The exception that matters in December is in Revenue and Taxation Code section 17946, repeated in FTB Publication 3556 and the Form 568 instructions. An LLC isn't subject to the annual tax and fee if **both** are true:

- its taxable year was **15 days or less**, and
- it **did no business in California** during that taxable year.

For a calendar-year LLC, December 17 through December 31 is exactly 15 days. So filing on **December 17 or later**, and then doing nothing until January, keeps the 2026 stub year inside the rule.

![California LLC in December 2026: filed December 10 gives a 22-day 2026 taxable year that owes the $800 annual tax; filed December 17 or later with no business gives a taxable year of 15 days or less that owes nothing for 2026](assets/images/llc-california-15-day-rule.webp "California's 15-day rule for a calendar-year LLC. Both conditions must hold: 15 days or less and no business in California. Source: R&TC section 17946; FTB Pub. 3556.")

Miss the window and the cost shows up fast. FTB Publication 3556 says a new LLC's first-year tax is due by the **15th day of the 4th month** after it files its Articles (its example: filed June 19, due September 15). An LLC filed on December 10, 2026 would owe the 2026 tax by March 15, 2027, and then the 2027 tax by April 15, 2027. That's $1,600 within about a month, for a business that may have earned nothing yet.

Two cautions. "Did no business" means just that: no sales, contracts, or other activity in California during those days. And California has other details, such as the separate LLC fee based on income, so if the $800 matters to you, confirm your dates with the FTB or a California tax professional.

# Texas: a delayed effective date on Form 205

The Texas Secretary of State's Certificate of Formation for an LLC (Form 205, filing fee **$300**) has an "Effectiveness of Filing" section. You can choose for the document to take effect when filed, or at a later date, or a later date and time, **not more than 90 days from the date of signing**. So a Texas certificate signed in November or December can name January 1, 2027.

The Texas form doesn't promise any particular fee saving, and we haven't checked how a January start affects Texas franchise tax reports. Treat the effective date as a way to line up the LLC's legal start with the day you open, and check the Comptroller's rules for the reports that follow.

**Other states:** many formation forms have a similar effective-date box, but the limits and the fee effects differ. Look for "effective date" on your state's form before you file. Our [[llc-cost-breakdown|LLC cost breakdown]] lists the filing fees and annual costs state by state.

# The federal side: one owner versus partners

**Single-member LLC.** Because the LLC is disregarded, the formation date doesn't create a separate federal return. Any business income and expenses go on your own return for the year they happen. If you do earn money in December, remember the last 2026 estimated tax payment is due January 15, 2027. See [[estimated-taxes-first-year-business|estimated taxes in your first year]].

**Multi-member LLC.** By default it's a partnership and files **Form 1065**. Under the Form 1065 instructions, a domestic partnership must file unless it **neither receives income nor incurs any expenditures treated as deductions or credits**. For a calendar-year partnership, the return is due **March 15**. The late-filing penalty in the current (2025) instructions is **$255 per partner, per month**, for up to 12 months. So a two-member LLC that starts on December 20, 2026 and has any income or deductible expenses before December 31 would need a 2026 Form 1065 by March 15, 2027. An LLC whose existence starts January 1, 2027 has no 2026 partnership year at all.

**Start-up costs follow the business, not the paperwork.** IRS Publication 583 says you can elect to deduct up to **$5,000 of start-up costs** and up to **$5,000 of organizational costs**. Each $5,000 is reduced by the amount your costs go over **$50,000**, and the rest is amortized. The Form 4562 instructions say the deduction is for the year your business **begins**, and the remainder is spread over **180 months** from the month you begin operations. Filing the LLC in December doesn't move that year if you actually open in January.

# Two things that don't depend on the date

- **The EIN.** The IRS asks you to form the entity with your state first, then apply for the EIN. If you chose a January 1 effective date, the simplest order is to apply once the LLC exists. It's free and takes minutes online. See [[ein-number-free|how to get an EIN for free]].
- **FinCEN's BOI report.** You don't need to file one. FinCEN's final rule of August 11, 2026, effective August 14, 2026, says companies created in the United States are exempt from beneficial ownership information reporting. It made permanent an interim rule from March 2025.

# File now or wait: a quick checklist

**File now, effective now,** if you'll take customers, sign a lease, or open the business bank account in December. Liability protection and a clean bank trail are worth more than one year of state fees.

**File now with a January 1 effective date** (Florida, Texas, and other states that allow it), if you're ready but won't operate until January. Your name is on file now, and the company's first year is 2027.

**Wait, or file late in the month,** in California, if you want to avoid the 2026 $800. File on or after December 17 and do no business until January, or simply file in January.

**Partners?** Remember that a December start can mean a 2026 Form 1065 due March 15, 2027.

Once the LLC exists, the next steps are usually the EIN, the bank account, and any licenses. See [[business-license-do-i-need|whether you need a business license]].
