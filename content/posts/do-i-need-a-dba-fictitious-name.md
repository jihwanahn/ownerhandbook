**Short answer:** you need a **DBA** — also called an **assumed name**, **fictitious business name**, or **trade name** — only when you regularly do business under a name that is **not** your legal name. For a sole proprietor, that means any name other than your full legal name. For an LLC, that means any name other than the exact name on your formation filing. If you invoice, advertise, and bank under that legal name, you generally **do not** file a DBA. A DBA is a **public notice** filing. It is **not** an LLC, it does **not** create liability protection by itself, and in most states it does **not** give you exclusive rights to the name.

![Do you need a DBA: yes when the trade name differs from your legal name; no when you operate under your full legal name or the LLC's exact formation name](assets/images/dba-do-you-need.webp "A DBA is required only for a name that is not your legal name. It is a notice filing, not an LLC or a trademark. Sources: SBA; Florida Division of Corporations.")

This guide is for sole proprietors and single-member LLC owners who are choosing a public-facing name before year-end or before opening a business bank account. Rules differ by state. We cover the test that applies everywhere, then the filing offices we checked in Florida, California, Texas, and New York.

# What a DBA is — and what it is not

The U.S. Small Business Administration groups business-name registrations into four separate tracks: the **entity name** with your state, a **federal trademark**, a **DBA**, and a **domain name**. Each does a different job. The SBA's page on choosing a business name says registering a DBA **does not provide legal protection by itself**, but **most states require you to register** a DBA if you use one.

Three filings get confused constantly:

| Filing | What it does | What it does not do |
|---|---|---|
| **DBA / assumed / fictitious name** | Puts the public on notice that you trade under another name | Create an entity, shield personal assets, or (in most states) reserve exclusive rights to the name |
| **LLC or corporation formation** | Creates a separate legal entity with the state | Give you a second brand name unless that name is the one on the filing |
| **Federal trademark (USPTO)** | Protects a brand for listed goods or services nationwide | Replace a state or county DBA filing |

![A DBA is a name record, not a company: DBA is public notice; LLC formation creates the entity; a USPTO trademark protects the brand nationally](assets/images/dba-not-an-llc.webp "Three filings owners mix up. A DBA does not create liability protection. Sources: SBA; Florida Division of Corporations; USPTO.")

Florida's Division of Corporations states the same limit plainly: registering a fictitious name does **not** grant ownership or rights to the name, and does **not** stop someone else from using or registering the same fictitious name. Texas's Secretary of State says the same for assumed-name certificates: the filing is notice, not a right to block others.

A DBA also does **not** replace a [[business-license-do-i-need|business license]], a sales-tax permit, or an [[ein-number-free|EIN]]. Those are separate steps.

# The one-question test

Ask: **is the name on the door, the invoice, the website, and the bank account identical to the legal name?**

- **Sole proprietor.** Your legal name is your own full personal name. "Maria Gonzalez Consulting" is a DBA. "Maria Gonzalez" is not.
- **LLC or corporation.** Your legal name is the exact name on the Articles or Certificate of Formation, including the LLC or Inc. designator your state requires. "River Oak LLC" trading as "River Oak Design" needs a DBA. "River Oak LLC" trading as "River Oak LLC" does not.

Florida's Fictitious Name Act page defines a fictitious name as a name different from your personal name (sole prop) or different from the entity's legal name (LLC or corporation), and says registration of a fictitious name is **not required** for an individual's legal name or for the name of a corporation, LLC, or limited partnership already on file. That pattern — file only when the trade name differs — is the rule in the states we checked.

**Why banks ask for it anyway.** The SBA notes that getting a DBA and an EIN is what many owners use to open a business bank account in the trade name. Florida's page says most banks also require proof of registration when opening an account. If you plan to deposit checks made out to the brand name, expect the bank to ask for the DBA filing even when you already have an LLC under a different legal name.

# Florida, California, Texas, New York: who files where

The idea is the same; the **office**, **fee**, and **extra steps** are not. Here is what each state's own pages say.

![Where you file a DBA in Florida, California, Texas, and New York: state Sunbiz, county clerk, SOS Form 503 or county, and DOS or county](assets/images/dba-four-states.webp "Same concept, different filing office. Sources: FL DOS; CA Business and Professions Code Chapter 5; TX SOS Name Filings FAQs and Form 503; NY DOS Certificate of Assumed Name.")

## Florida — state filing on Sunbiz

- File a **Fictitious Name Registration** with the Florida Division of Corporations (Sunbiz) **before** conducting business under that name (Fictitious Name Act, s. 865.09, F.S.).
- **Fee:** $50 for registration.
- **Term:** 5 years; expires December 31 of the final year; renewable.
- You must advertise the name **at least once** in a newspaper in the county of your principal place of business. Proof of the ad is not filed; you certify it when you sign.
- **Not required** for your legal personal name, or for the exact name of an LLC, corporation, or limited partnership already on file and not trading under any other name.
- Failure to register when required is a second-degree misdemeanor under Florida law.

## California — county clerk, then publish

- California calls it a **fictitious business name statement**. Business and Professions Code section 17910 says anyone who regularly transacts business in the state for profit under a fictitious business name must file **not later than 40 days** after starting under that name.
- File with the **clerk of the county** where you have your principal place of business in California (BPC §17915). If you have no California place of business, file with the Clerk of Sacramento County.
- After filing, publish the statement in a newspaper of general circulation in that county **once a week for four consecutive weeks**, starting within 45 days of filing (BPC §17917; Government Code §6064). File the affidavit of publication with the county clerk within 45 days after publication ends.
- A statement generally **expires five years** from the filing date (BPC §17920); refile before it expires.
- County filing fees vary. Check your county clerk's fee schedule.

## Texas — Secretary of State for LLCs; county for sole props

- Texas uses **assumed name certificates** under Business & Commerce Code Chapter 71.
- **LLCs, corporations, limited partnerships, LLPs, and foreign filing entities** file Form **503** with the **Secretary of State**. Fee: **$25**. Term: up to **10 years**. Since September 1, 2019 (HB 3609), these entities do **not** also file at the county level.
- **Sole proprietors** (and general partnerships) file with the **county clerk** in each county where they maintain a business office, or where they conduct business if they have no Texas office. County fees vary.
- An assumed name cannot be the entity's **exact** legal name; the SOS will reject that. Filing does not block others from filing the same assumed name.

## New York — DOS for LLCs; county for sole props

- **LLCs** that want to conduct activities under a name other than their true legal name file a **Certificate of Assumed Name** with the New York Department of State. Filing fee: **$25** (Limited Liability Company Law §204(c); General Business Law §130).
- **Sole proprietors** file an assumed-name certificate with the **county clerk** where the business operates. New York Business Express lists the basic fee as **$25** in all counties except the five New York City counties, where it is **$100**.

**Other states:** many use "assumed name," "fictitious name," or "trade name." Some file only with the state, some only with the county, some with both. Search your Secretary of State or county clerk for the phrase your state uses before you pay a third-party service.

# How a DBA fits the rest of your paperwork

A DBA sits next to formation, tax IDs, and licenses — it does not replace them.

1. **Decide the public name.** If it matches your legal name, skip the DBA.
2. **Form the entity if you want one.** An LLC is optional for a sole prop who only needs a trade name. If you are forming late in the year, see [[starting-llc-in-december|starting an LLC in December]]. Formation fees and annual costs are covered in the [[llc-cost-breakdown|LLC cost breakdown]].
3. **File the DBA** with the right state or county office if the trade name differs.
4. **Get an EIN** when you need one for banking, hiring, or the IRS rules that apply to your structure. A DBA by itself does not require a new EIN. See [[ein-number-free|how to get an EIN for free]].
5. **Open the business bank account** in the name the bank will accept — legal name, DBA, or both — using the formation documents, EIN letter, and DBA proof they ask for.
6. **Handle licenses and tax permits** separately. A DBA is not a [[business-license-do-i-need|business license]].

# Year-end timing

If you plan to take payments or deposit checks under a brand name in December or January, file the DBA **before** those checks arrive. Florida requires registration before conducting business under the name. California gives you 40 days after you start, but banks and platforms often want the stamped filing up front. Newspaper publication in Florida and California adds calendar days, so do not leave it for the week you open.

A DBA does not change your federal tax classification. A sole proprietor with a DBA still reports on Schedule C. A single-member LLC still defaults to disregarded-entity treatment unless it elects otherwise.

# Checklist

- Confirm whether the name on invoices and the website matches your legal name.
- If it does not, identify the correct office: state, county, or both.
- Search existing records (Sunbiz, county index, SOS assumed-name search) before you brand around a name.
- Budget for the filing fee **and** any required newspaper notice.
- Keep the stamped filing; banks and local license offices ask for it.
- Remember: a DBA is not liability protection and not a trademark.
