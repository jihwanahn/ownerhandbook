**Short answer:** for 2026, a payment app or online marketplace has to send you a **Form 1099-K** only if the payments you received for goods or services through that platform total **more than $20,000 and more than 200 transactions**. The planned **$600** threshold is gone. The One, Big, Beautiful Bill retroactively restored the old rule. **Card payments work differently.** If customers pay you by credit, debit or gift card through a card processor, you get a 1099-K **no matter how small the amount**. And whether or not a form shows up, **all of your business income goes on your tax return**.

![Will you get a Form 1099-K for 2026: card payments through a processor are reported at any amount; payment apps and online marketplaces report only over $20,000 and over 200 transactions; either way, all business income is reported on your return](assets/images/1099-k-2026-threshold.webp "Two kinds of 1099-K and two different thresholds for 2026. Sources: IRS, Understanding your Form 1099-K; IRS Fact Sheet 2025-08.")

This guide is for owners who take payments through apps, marketplaces or card terminals. That includes sole proprietors, single-member LLCs and small partnerships. It covers who sends the form, what the number on it really means, and what to do when it's wrong.

# Two kinds of payments, two different rules

Form 1099-K covers two separate channels, and the threshold depends on which one paid you.

**Payment cards.** These are credit, debit and stored-value cards such as gift cards. If your customers pay you directly by card, your payment card processor must report those payments with no minimum. The IRS FAQ puts it plainly: if you received **$0.01** through payment card transactions, you should get a Form 1099-K.

**Payment apps and online marketplaces.** The IRS calls these **third party settlement organizations (TPSOs)**. They include payment apps, online and craft marketplaces, auction sites, ride-hailing and car-sharing platforms, ticket resale sites, crowdfunding platforms and freelance marketplaces. A TPSO must file a 1099-K only when **both** of these are true for the year:

- the gross payments you received through that platform for goods or services are **over $20,000**, and
- there were **more than 200 transactions**.

Both conditions have to be met. $25,000 in 150 transactions doesn't require a form, and neither does $8,000 in 900 transactions. The test is applied **per platform**, so if you sell on two marketplaces, each one counts only its own payments. You could get one form, two, or none.

Two cautions. A platform **may still send you a 1099-K** below the threshold; the IRS says so on its own page. And **your state may have a lower threshold**, which can produce a form even when the federal test isn't met.

**Bank transfers aren't in this system.** The IRS FAQ says an automated clearing house (ACH), which handles direct deposits, wire transfers and electronic checks, is **not** a TPSO. Payments a client sends straight to your bank account aren't reported on a 1099-K. They're still income, of course.

# What happened to the $600 threshold

Under the American Rescue Plan Act of 2021, TPSOs were supposed to report any payee who received more than **$600** for goods or services, with no transaction count. The IRS delayed that change several times through transition notices. Then the One, Big, Beautiful Bill **retroactively reinstated** the earlier rule: over $20,000 and over 200 transactions. The IRS's current Form 1099-K pages and its October 2025 fact sheet (FS-2025-08) state the $20,000 and 200 threshold, and the IRS's guidance for gig workers repeats it.

So if you read a few years ago that anyone with $600 in app sales would get a 1099-K, that's no longer the federal rule.

# When the form arrives and what's on it

Card companies, payment apps and marketplaces must send you your copy **by January 31**, and they file a copy with the IRS. For 2026 payments, that means by January 31, 2027.

The key number is **Box 1a, the gross payment amount**. It's the total value of the payments you received, and the IRS says it **isn't adjusted** for any of these:

- fees
- credits
- refunds
- shipping
- cash equivalents
- discounts

That's why Box 1a is almost always **bigger than what you actually kept**. The IRS says those items aren't taxable income and you can subtract them from the gross amount. Your platform's reports, merchant statements and receipts are what back that up.

![Example of reconciling a Form 1099-K: Box 1a shows $24,000 gross; subtract $700 platform fees, $500 refunds and $800 shipping charged to buyers to reach the amounts you check against your books; the form's gross is not your profit](assets/images/1099-k-gross-to-books.webp "Example numbers only. Box 1a is gross and isn't reduced for fees, refunds, shipping, discounts or other adjustments. Source: IRS, What to do with Form 1099-K.")

Where each of those items lands on your return depends on your books and your tax software. The goal is the same either way: your return should account for the full Box 1a figure, and your records should explain the difference between it and your real income.

# Where 1099-K income goes on your return

The IRS splits this by how your business is set up:

- **Sole proprietor, gig worker or freelancer**, which includes a **single-member LLC** taxed the default way: report it on **Schedule C (Form 1040)**. The Schedule C instructions list Form 1099-K among the forms whose amounts belong there.
- **Partnership:** the IRS's page lists **Schedule E (Form 1040)**. The partnership itself files Form 1065.
- **Corporation or S corporation:** Form 1120 or Form 1120-S.

**Watch the name and number on the form.** If a 1099-K carries your personal name and Social Security number, but your business files Form 1065, 1120 or 1120-S, the IRS says you need to get the form **corrected**. The same goes when you switch entities. If you go from a sole proprietorship to a partnership and keep using the same card terminal, the form won't match your new entity's return. The IRS tells you to update the name and tax ID with your processor right away. The easiest fix is to give each platform the right taxpayer number from the start. Our guides on [[how-to-fill-out-w9-single-member-llc|filling out a W-9 as a single-member LLC]] and [[ein-number-free|getting a free EIN]] cover which number to use.

# When the form is wrong

The IRS **can't** correct a Form 1099-K for you. The fixes all go through the company that issued it.

**You got a form you shouldn't have.** This includes personal payments such as gifts, a friend paying back their share of dinner, or a roommate covering rent, as well as a form that isn't yours or duplicates another. Contact the filer named in the top-left corner of the form and ask for a **corrected 1099-K showing zero**. Keep the original and your correspondence. In payment apps, the IRS suggests marking personal payments as non-business whenever you can.

**The amount or the taxpayer number is wrong.** Ask the filer for a corrected form. If you don't recognize the filer, contact the payment settlement entity listed in the bottom-left corner, above your account number.

**Don't hold your return waiting for it.** The IRS says to file anyway. If the gross amount is wrong, report the amount from the incorrect form at the **top of Schedule 1 (Form 1040)**. That's the entry space the IRS points to for this case.

**A few business-specific cases** the IRS addresses:

- **Shared card terminal.** If someone else ran sales through your terminal, their payments are in your Box 1a. The IRS says to file and furnish the appropriate information return to them where required, and keep the agreements and payment records.
- **Debit card cash back.** Cash you hand customers at checkout shows up on the 1099-K, but it generally isn't part of your gross receipts and isn't a business expense either. Keep a record of it.
- **Business bought or sold mid-year.** If the terminal wasn't updated, the form can include the other owner's sales. Ask for a corrected form and keep the sale agreement that shows the date.

# If you pay contractors through an app or card

This rule runs the other way too, and it can save you paperwork. The IRS's instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC say that payments made **with a credit card or payment card**, and certain other payments including **third-party network transactions**, are reported on Form 1099-K by the payment settlement entity. They are **not** reported on Form 1099-NEC or 1099-MISC.

So if you paid a freelancer $3,000 through a payment platform that acts as a TPSO, or by credit card, you generally don't issue them a 1099-NEC for those payments. Payments by check, cash or direct bank transfer are a different matter. For those, see [[do-i-need-to-send-1099-to-llc|whether you need to send a 1099 to an LLC]], and if you do owe forms, [[how-to-e-file-1099-nec-iris|how to e-file 1099-NEC for free with IRIS]].

# No form doesn't mean no tax

This is the point people miss most often now that the threshold went back up. The IRS says that **whether or not you get a Form 1099-K**, you must report all business income, including payments in cash, property, goods or digital assets. A seller with $15,000 in marketplace sales may never see a 1099-K, but that $15,000 still belongs on Schedule C.

If your app and marketplace income is growing, plan for the tax during the year rather than in April. Our guide to [[estimated-taxes-first-year-business|estimated taxes in your first year]] explains the quarterly payments. The last one for 2026 is due January 15, 2027.

# Quick checklist for January

- Download the year-end sales and fee reports from every platform and processor you use.
- When each 1099-K arrives, compare Box 1a with your own gross sales for that platform.
- Check the name and taxpayer number against the return your business files.
- Ask the filer for a correction if something is wrong, but don't delay your return.
- Report all business income, with or without a form.
