**Short answer:** if your holiday helper is an **employee** rather than an independent contractor, you need an **EIN**, a signed **Form W-4**, and a **Form I-9**. The employee completes I-9 Section 1 by the first day of paid work, and you complete Section 2 **within three business days**. You then **report the hire to your state within 20 days** (some states require it sooner), withhold and deposit payroll taxes, and give the worker a **2026 Form W-2 by February 1, 2027**. That date applies because January 31, 2027 falls on a Sunday. Neither the W-4 nor the I-9 is mailed to the government; you keep both on file.

This guide covers the federal steps for a small business hiring one or a few **nonfarm, non-household** workers for the 2026 holiday season. Farmworkers and household employees follow different IRS rules, and every state adds its own registrations, so treat this as the federal backbone and check your state as you go.

# 1. Decide: employee or independent contractor?

Everything else depends on this answer, and a short seasonal job does not settle it. The IRS uses the common-law test: a worker is generally your employee **if you have the right to control what will be done and how it will be done**, even if you give them freedom in practice ([IRS Publication 15, section 2](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p15)).

The IRS groups the evidence into three categories on its [employee or independent contractor page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/independent-contractor-self-employed-or-employee):

| Category | The question to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Behavioral | Do you control, or have the right to control, what the worker does and how they do it? |
| Financial | Do you control the business side, such as how they are paid, whether expenses are reimbursed, and who provides tools and supplies? |
| Type of relationship | Are there written contracts or employee-type benefits, and is the work a key part of your business? |

No single factor decides it, and the IRS asks you to weigh the whole relationship and document how you reached your answer. A seasonal cashier or stockroom helper working the shifts you set, at your counter, with your register, points toward employee status on the behavioral and financial questions. If you still can't tell, either you or the worker can file **Form SS-8** for an official IRS determination, but the IRS says that can take at least six months, which is longer than most holiday seasons.

Getting this wrong is expensive. If you treat an employee as a contractor without a reasonable basis, the IRS says you can be held liable for the employment taxes on that worker. If the person really is an independent contractor, you generally don't withhold or pay employment taxes; see our guide on [[do-i-need-to-send-1099-to-llc|when you need to send a Form 1099]] for the year-end reporting side.

# 2. Before day one: EIN and state accounts

**Get an EIN if you don't have one.** Publication 15 says you need an employer identification number if you're required to report employment taxes or give tax statements to employees, and that you shouldn't use your SSN in its place. The IRS's [online EIN application](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/get-an-employer-identification-number) is free; our [[ein-number-free|EIN guide]] walks through it.

**Open your state employer accounts.** This is where the federal checklist ends and your state begins. The SBA's [hire and manage employees guide](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/manage-your-business/hire-manage-employees) lists finding out whether you need state or local tax IDs as an early payroll step, and notes that state employment obligations often include unemployment insurance tax, workers' compensation insurance, and, in some states, temporary disability insurance. It also says you may need to register with your state workforce agency for unemployment insurance and that you might be responsible for withholding state income tax. Ask your state tax agency whether it requires its own withholding form in addition to the federal W-4. The SBA page links to each state's department of revenue.

**Set the pay rate.** The federal minimum wage under the Fair Labor Standards Act is **$7.25 an hour**. The [Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/minimum-wage) notes that many states have their own minimum wage laws and that employers must comply with both, so the higher rate wins.

# 3. Day one: Form W-4 and Form I-9

**Form W-4.** The [IRS hiring employees page](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/hiring-employees) says to ask every new employee for a signed Form W-4 when they start, effective with the first paycheck, and to keep it on file. Use the **2026 Form W-4** for anyone you hire this year. If a new employee doesn't give you a completed W-4, Publication 15 says to withhold as if they checked **Single or Married filing separately** and made no other entries. You don't send W-4s to the IRS unless the IRS asks for them.

Record each employee's name and **Social Security number** as shown on their Social Security card ([Publication 15](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p15)), because both go on the W-2. The IRS says not to accept an ITIN in place of an SSN for employment.

**Form I-9.** Every U.S. employer must complete Form I-9 for every person hired, citizens included ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/hiring-employees); [USCIS](https://www.uscis.gov/i-9)). The timing comes from [USCIS I-9 Central](https://www.uscis.gov/i-9-central/completing-form-i-9/completing-section-2-employer-review-and-attestation):

- **Section 1:** the employee completes and signs it **no later than the first day of work for pay**. They can do it earlier, but not before accepting your job offer.
- **Section 2:** you, or someone you authorize, must examine the employee's **original, unexpired documents** and complete Section 2 **within three business days** of the first day of work for pay. USCIS's example: someone who starts on Monday must have Section 2 completed by Thursday.
- **Very short jobs:** if the job lasts **less than three business days**, Section 2 is due **no later than the first day of work**. That matters for one-weekend event help.

The employee chooses which documents to show: one from List A, or one from List B plus one from List C. USCIS says you can't tell them which documents to bring, and if they present an acceptable List A document, you can't ask for List B or C.

**Keep the I-9, don't mail it.** [USCIS](https://www.uscis.gov/i-9) says not to file Form I-9 with USCIS or ICE. Keep it for **three years after the date of hire or one year after employment ends, whichever is later**, and be ready to produce it within three business days of an inspection request. For a worker who stays less than two years, such as a holiday-only hire, USCIS says to keep it three years after the first day of employment ([USCIS retention guidance](https://www.uscis.gov/i-9-central/form-i-9-resources/handbook-for-employers-m-274/100-retaining-form-i-9)).

# 4. Within 20 days: the state new-hire report

Federal law requires employers to report new and rehired employees **within 20 days of hire to the state where the employee works**, and some states require it sooner ([HHS Administration for Children and Families](https://acf.gov/ocse/programs-services/employers/employer-responsibilities/new-hire-reporting)). The report feeds child support enforcement, and the seven federally required items are:

1. Employee's name
2. Employee's address
3. Employee's Social Security number
4. Date of hire, meaning the first day the employee performs services for pay
5. Your business name
6. Your business address
7. Your federal EIN

States may require more, and they set their own reporting methods; ACF maintains a list of [state new-hire reporting websites](https://acf.gov/ocse/data/map/state-new-hire-reporting-websites). If a seasonal worker comes back next year, ACF's guidance says to report them again if they were separated for **at least 60 consecutive days** or must complete a new W-4. Federal law doesn't require reporting independent contractors, though ACF notes that some states do.

# 5. Payroll taxes: withhold, deposit, file

Once paychecks start, the IRS says you generally must withhold income tax, Social Security tax, and Medicare tax from the employee's wages, pay the matching employer share, and pay federal unemployment (FUTA) tax ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/independent-contractor-self-employed-or-employee)). The 2026 numbers from [Publication 15](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p15):

| Tax | 2026 rate | Who pays |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Social Security | 6.2% each, up to $184,500 of wages | Employee (withheld) and employer |
| Medicare | 1.45% each, no wage limit | Employee (withheld) and employer |
| Federal income tax | Based on the employee's Form W-4 (see IRS Pub. 15-T) | Employee (withheld) |
| FUTA | 6.0% on the first $7,000 per employee, generally 0.6% after the state unemployment credit | Employer only |

**When to deposit.** Publication 15 says a new Form 941 filer is a **monthly schedule depositor for its first calendar year**. Under the monthly schedule, taxes on wages paid during a month are due by the **15th of the following month**. For holiday payroll, that means taxes on November 2026 paychecks are due by **December 15, 2026**, and taxes on December paychecks by **January 15, 2027**. If a deposit date falls on a weekend or legal holiday, the deposit moves to the next business day. Deposits must be made **electronically**: Publication 15 lists EFTPS, IRS Direct Pay, and your IRS business tax account as free options, or a payroll service can deposit for you. Publication 15 also has an exception for small quarterly totals: if your Form 941 tax liability for the current quarter or the prior quarter is under **$2,500**, you may be able to pay with a timely filed return instead. The IRS cautions that if you're unsure you'll stay under $2,500, you should deposit on schedule to avoid a failure-to-deposit penalty. The same section also has a $100,000 next-day deposit rule, which is unlikely for a few seasonal workers but worth knowing exists.

**Form 941.** File your first quarterly Form 941 for the quarter in which you first paid wages. For October to December 2026, the return is due by the last day of the following month, which moves to **February 1, 2027** because January 31 is a Sunday. Publication 15 lets you file by the 10th day of the second month after the quarter if all of the quarter's taxes were deposited on time. After your first Form 941, you must file every quarter, even with no wages, unless you're a **seasonal employer**. The [Form 941 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i941) say seasonal employers don't have to file for quarters in which they paid no wages, as long as they check the **seasonal employer box on line 18 of every Form 941 they file**.

**Form 940 (FUTA).** Under Publication 15's general test, you owe FUTA tax for 2026 if you paid **$1,500 or more in wages in any calendar quarter** of 2025 or 2026, or had at least one employee for some part of a day in **20 or more different weeks** in either year. A seasonal payroll that crosses $1,500 in the fourth quarter meets the test. Form 940 is due January 31, so the same weekend rule makes it **February 1, 2027** for 2026. FUTA tax of $500 or less for a quarter can be carried forward; if your fourth-quarter liability, plus any carried-forward amount, is over $500, deposit the full amount by the Form 940 due date.

# 6. After the season: Form W-2 by February 1, 2027

The [2026 General Instructions for Forms W-2 and W-3](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/iw2w3) set **February 1, 2027** as the deadline both to **furnish Form W-2 to employees** and to **file Copy A with Form W-3 with the Social Security Administration**, on paper or electronically. If a seasonal worker's job ends before December 31, 2026, you may give them their W-2 any time after employment ends, but no later than February 1, 2027. If they ask for it, the IRS says to provide it within 30 days of the request or of the final wage payment, whichever is later.

Keep all employment tax records for **at least four years**, which is the IRS minimum in Publication 15.

# 7. Hiring teenagers for the holidays

If your seasonal help includes minors, federal child labor rules apply on top of everything above. In nonfarm jobs, the [DOL's Fact Sheet #43](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/43-child-labor-non-agriculture) says 14- and 15-year-olds may work only outside school hours, up to **3 hours on a school day**, **8 hours on a non-school day**, **18 hours in a school week**, and **40 hours in a non-school week**, and only between **7 a.m. and 7 p.m.** outside the June 1 to Labor Day period. Workers aged 16 and 17 have no federal hour limits but can't do jobs the Labor Department has declared hazardous. Children under 14 generally can't be employed in nonfarm jobs covered by the FLSA. Many states add their own rules and work permits, and when both apply, the **stricter standard** governs.

# The short checklist

1. Decide employee or contractor and write down why.
2. Get an EIN, then open state withholding and unemployment accounts.
3. On day one, collect a 2026 Form W-4 and I-9 Section 1, plus any state withholding form.
4. Complete I-9 Section 2 within three business days, or on day one for jobs under three days.
5. File the state new-hire report within 20 days, or sooner if your state requires.
6. Deposit payroll taxes by the 15th of the following month.
7. By February 1, 2027: W-2s to workers and SSA, the fourth-quarter Form 941, and Form 940 if FUTA applies.

If the holiday business is also your first year of profit, our guide to [[estimated-taxes-first-year-business|estimated taxes in your first year]] covers the owner's side of the tax bill, which payroll withholding does not cover.
